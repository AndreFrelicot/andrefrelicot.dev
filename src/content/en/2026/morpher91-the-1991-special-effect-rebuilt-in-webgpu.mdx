## 1991, the year pixels learned to melt

Two images defined early-90s special effects. The T-1000 in *Terminator 2*,
liquid metal reshaping itself into a human. And the final sequence of Michael
Jackson's *Black or White*: faces transforming into one another, smoothly,
without a cut. Both date from 1991. Both rest on the same idea, **morphing**:
not fading two images into each other, but *deforming each toward the other*
while blending them.

The technique behind *Black or White* — the *field morphing* of Thaddeus Beier
and Shawn Neely, published at SIGGRAPH 1992 — ran on Silicon Graphics
workstations back then, far beyond the reach of most people. Thirty-five years later, the
whole pipeline fits in a browser tab: deformation fields are evaluated per pixel on
the GPU through WebGPU, video is encoded to H.264 through WebCodecs, and nothing
ever leaves the machine.

**Morpher91** was born from that: an **educational 2D morphing studio** where
you import two images or videos, place points, lines and regions, compare the
algorithms, compose layers and masks, then export the result as MP4. No
SaaS, no account, no backend: a tech demo, with this article explaining how it was
built.

The **layers** are my favorite part: a face deformed by lines, hair by points,
a background with a simple crossfade, and each zone keeps its landmarks, its mask
and its own rhythm.

This devlog covers the ideas and the decisions: the **mathematics** of the four
algorithms, their **layered composition**, the **WebGPU pipeline** shared by
preview and export, the **video timeline** and its serialized seeks,
the **scrubbing pipeline** that ended up using a WebCodecs decoder, the
**keyframes** you manipulate directly on the timeline, and surviving on
**iPad**.

## The principle: warp toward a common shape, then dissolve

Let's start with what does not work. The **crossfade** — linearly interpolating
pixels, `mix(A, B, t)` — produces **ghosting**: halfway through, both sets of
contours coexist, two noses, four eyes, a ghostly superposition. It is the
artifact everyone has seen in slideshows.

All of morphing fits in one sentence:

> **Warp both images toward a common intermediate shape, then dissolve.**

At time `t`, you compute an *intermediate* geometry (the points of A moved
toward those of B, a fraction `t` of the way there), you deform A **forward** toward that
shape, you deform B **backward** toward that same shape, and only then do you
blend the two results. The contours coincide at every instant, and the ghosts
disappear. Well, wherever the landmarks are good: an ear hidden in only one of
the two images will still produce a doubled outline.

The whole question then becomes: *how* do you define that deformation? This is
where the algorithm families diverge — and it is exactly what the studio's
Compare view lets you observe, on the same image pairs.

## Four families, one shared pipeline

### Crossfade — the baseline that shows the problem

Deliberately kept in the app as a point of comparison. One shader, one
`mix()`. Its pedagogical role is to make ghosting visible so the three other
methods have something to beat.

### Mesh — triangulate and project

The VHS-era method: place paired **landmarks** on A and B (the *features* in
the studio's vocabulary), triangulate their halfway shape (Delaunay, via
[Delaunator](https://github.com/mapbox/delaunator)), then keep that
connectivity as long as the landmarks do not change. You interpolate the
vertices at time `t`, then project each triangle of A and of B toward the
intermediate triangle through an affine transform. Fast, robust, fully
parallel — but the deformation is *piecewise* affine: on large motions, you can
see the mesh edges folding the image like paper.

### Thin-Plate Spline — the metal plate you bend

TPS answers the question: *what is the smoothest possible deformation that
passes exactly through my control points?* The analogy is physical: a thin
plate forced through the landmarks while minimizing bending energy. The
interpolation is written as an affine part plus a weighted sum of a radial
kernel:

$$
f(\mathbf{p}) = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 y + \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i \, U\!\left(\lVert \mathbf{p} - \mathbf{p}_i \rVert\right),
\qquad U(r) = r^2 \log r
$$

```ts
function tpsKernel(r: number): number {
  if (r < 1e-6) return 0;
  return r * r * Math.log(r);
}
```

The CPU/GPU split is the heart of the architectural decision: the weights
`w_i` come from a **small dense linear system** (a few dozen landmarks), solved
on the **CPU** for the two inverse paths, from the intermediate shape toward A
and toward B. Since that shape moves with `t`, there is not one solution but
one per position: `t` is quantized to 1/256 and an LRU cache keeps the last 64,
flushed as soon as a landmark or a setting changes. The **field evaluation**,
on the other hand, happens **per pixel on the GPU**:

```wgsl
fn tps_kernel(r: f32) -> f32 {
  if (r < 0.000001) {
    return 0.0;
  }
  return r * r * log(r);
}
```

Two guardrails keep the preview stable: **border anchors** (implicit landmarks
at the corners and edges, so the image does not detach from its frame) and a
**regularization** parameter λ added to the matrix diagonal, trading a bit of
interpolation exactness for a lot of smoothness when the control points are
noisy.

### Beier–Neely — the lines of *Black or White*

The method closest to the 1991 spirit works not with points but with **feature
lines**: the ridge of the nose, the jawline, the contour of an eyebrow. Each
pair of lines defines a local frame; a pixel is expressed in the frame of each
destination line, then reprojected through the corresponding source line. The
influences combine through a weighted average:

$$
X' = X + \frac{\sum_i w_i \,(X'_i - X)}{\sum_i w_i},
\qquad
w_i = \left( \frac{l_i^{\,p}}{a + d_i} \right)^{b}
$$

where `l` is the line length, `d` the distance from the pixel to the line, and
`p`, `a`, `b` the three knobs of *field morphing*: influence of length,
smoothness, concentration. In the shader, a single loop evaluates both warps
(toward A and toward B) on the same set of interpolated lines: the distance and
the weight are computed only once per line, and `u.coeffs` stores `a`, `b` and
`p` in that order.

```wgsl title="beierNeely.wgsl"
fn warp_both(x : vec2f, t : f32, count : u32) -> vec4f {
  var totalA = vec2f(0.0, 0.0);
  var totalB = vec2f(0.0, 0.0);
  var totalWeight = 0.0;

  for (var i = 0u; i < count; i = i + 1u) {
    let ln = lines[i];
    let a0 = ln.a.xy;
    let a1 = ln.a.zw;
    let b0 = ln.b.xy;
    let b1 = ln.b.zw;

    let m0 = mix(a0, b0, t);
    let m1 = mix(a1, b1, t);

    let dist = line_distance(x, m0, m1);
    let len = length(m1 - m0);
    let weight =
      pow(pow(len, u.coeffs.z) / (u.coeffs.x + dist), u.coeffs.y) * ln.params.x;

    let mappedA = map_point_by_line_pair(x, m0, m1, a0, a1);
    let mappedB = map_point_by_line_pair(x, m0, m1, b0, b1);

    totalA = totalA + (mappedA - x) * weight;
    totalB = totalB + (mappedB - x) * weight;
    totalWeight = totalWeight + weight;
  }

  if (totalWeight <= 0.000001) {
    return vec4f(x, x);
  }
  return vec4f(x + totalA / totalWeight, x + totalB / totalWeight);
}
```

It is expensive — every pixel visits every line — but it is exactly the kind of
brutal, regular loop GPUs love, and the result has that "organic" character
neither the mesh nor TPS reproduce: the lines *pull* the image around them.

## One space to rule them all: the Project Space

A fundamental decision, invisible on screen: **all coordinates live in a common
normalized project space**. Not source-media pixels (A and B almost never share
a size), not CSS pixels (zoom and pan change constantly), not canvas pixels
(render resolution varies with the device and the GPU budget).

You pair the nose of A with the nose of B once, and those positions stay valid
whatever the zoom or the format of the two images. The same JSON feeds the
preview, the debug views and the export, even when they do not render at the
same resolution. Every conversion goes through dedicated functions — it is
unexciting, it takes very little code, and it is probably the decision that prevented
more bugs than any other in the project.

## Layers are not a fifth algorithm

Nobody morphs a whole shot with a single field. In *Black or White*, the face,
the hair and the background do not transform at the same pace, and a setting
that suits the eyes drags the scenery along with the face. Hence the
**layers**: each one computes its own morph from the **same two sources A and
B**, with its landmarks, its algorithm if needed, and a mask that delimits what
it keeps of the result. A typical portrait combines three separate treatments:

- **The background in crossfade**, on the Global layer that serves as the base.
- **The hair in TPS**, with its control points and a mask to confine that
  deformation field to the hair.
- **The face in Beier–Neely**, with lines for the eyes, the nose and the jaw,
  and a soft-edged mask to blend it into the rest.

The three methods contribute to **the same final image**, and adjusting a
line on the face does not affect the TPS computation for the hair. The classic
trap: it is the mask that cuts out, not the landmarks. A layer without a mask
covers the whole frame and hides the ones below.

The separation also holds for time. Each layer has its block on the timeline, a
start and a duration, and inside it the **warp** and **dissolve** windows are
set separately, each with its own easing curve, up to a custom Bézier. The
background can therefore start its dissolve while the face is still warping,
without revealing B in the face layer yet. Mind the edges of the block: outside it, the
layer vanishes at once.

The stack order, the opacity and the blend mode then decide the superposition.
The compositor blends everything in **premultiplied alpha**, with the
`normal`, `multiply`, `screen` and `lighter` modes, and each layer can be
inspected alone in the debug views: its result, its mask, its contribution,
then the full stack to check the seams.

The renderer's golden rule: **the viewport, the debug views and the export
call the same `LayeredRenderer`**, with the same layer composition and the same
evaluation of the timeline. The preview sometimes runs at reduced resolution or on a
video proxy, and the encoder adds its compression, but there is only one
morph. There is no second version that could drift.

The layers' **vector masks** follow the same rule. Their outline, straight or
smoothed with Catmull–Rom, shares its geometry with the editing overlay; that
small geometry is triangulated on the CPU, then rasterized on the GPU in
`r8unorm` with **4× MSAA** — one byte per texel instead of four. The
**feathering** uses a separable multi-scale Gaussian blur, on the GPU as well.
Preview, debug and export build the mask the same way, with no CPU readback on
every frame.

### A painted mask that follows the face

The brush paints a raster mask on the source image. The trap
would be to leave it still while the features move: a cheek touch-up would end
up cutting out the nose. Each morph backend therefore knows how to produce an
**inverse deformation map**: for each pixel of the result, where to read in A?
The compositor samples the painted mask at that same coordinate. The paint
thus follows the layer's deformation without having to be repainted at every
instant.

A vector region and a painted mask can coexist on the same layer; their
coverage combines by union, the maximum of the two, before the optional
inversion. A surprise on the first try: the eraser does not erase what the
region covers. The "Combined masks — region and brush" preset shows it in the
Mask view.

### Warp first, dissolve at the right moment

Even with careful landmarks, varying the shape and the opacity at exactly the
same pace is not always the best choice. The **Classic morph** transition
preset warps over the whole duration, but concentrates the dissolve between
**30% and 70%**. The beginning gives A more time to approach the common shape
before B becomes visible; the end lets B finish its motion once A is gone. The
doubled contours are therefore visible for less time near the start and end of the transition. Each
window also has its own easing curve, with a Bézier editor for fine
adjustments.

## Then video arrived, and time with it

V1 targeted still images. But the iconic morphing — *Black or White*, again —
happens between **videos**: the subjects move *during* the morph. Adding it
brought in a graphics app's worst enemy: time.

Two A/B video sources, a **master timeline in seconds**, clips that are never
stretched, per-side landmark keyframes. And very quickly, competing clocks: the
video element has its own, `requestAnimationFrame` its own, the store its own.
The redesign imposed **a single RAF authority** — one loop owns τ, the master
time, and everyone else reads it.

The sneakiest problem: **stale seeks**. A video seek is asynchronous; during a
fast scrub, ten seek requests are issued and complete out of order, and a late response
can publish an old frame *on top of* the right one. The solution: serialize the
seeks and guard them with a **generation**:

```ts title="PreviewEngine.ts"
sync(tauSec: number, { realtime = false } = {}): Promise<void> {
  const generation = ++this.syncGeneration;
  const run = this.syncTail
    .catch(() => undefined)
    .then(async () => {
      if (generation !== this.syncGeneration) return;
      await this.applyFrame(tauSec, realtime, generation);
    });
  this.syncTail = run;
  return run;
}
```

Every request increments the counter; a response whose generation is no longer
the latest is simply discarded, *before* the GPU upload and *before*
display. Playback prioritizes real-time pacing (the videos play while we sample their frames); scrubbing
and export pause playback and seek the **exact frame**. Two modes, one engine.

One last choice to keep project files small: video bytes are **never** embedded in the project
file. Images live there as data URLs, but a video only leaves its metadata and
a lightweight fingerprint: name, size and modification date, or failing that the
same dimensions and a similar duration. On reopening, you point to the local
file again and the app checks that it matches. A project weighs what its
images weigh, not its videos: a few hundred kilobytes or a few megabytes, never
a one-gigabyte archive.

## Scrubbing, or fitting a video decoder under your thumb

A video timeline is judged by how well it scrubs: you grab the playhead, you drag it,
and the image must follow. The first version did not follow. It took two development milestones
to improve it, illustrating the gap between "it works" and "it is
instant".

The first eliminated unnecessary work. Every pointer event recomputed everything:
the temporal sampling of the landmarks, the triangulation, the TPS solve, the
overlay grids, then one render per surface. Today the sampling is memoized (τ
quantized to 1/240 s), redraws are coalesced to one per vsync and per surface,
React propagation is quantized to the frame, and a view whose frame has not
changed no longer launches any GPU pass.

The second tackled the decoding itself. Seeking a `<video>` element is slow
and imprecise; Morpher91 now reads the packets with
[Mediabunny](https://github.com/Vanilagy/mediabunny) and decodes them through
**WebCodecs**, into an LRU cache of GPU textures indexed by source frame
number. The diagnosis had its moment of humility: the demo clips were encoded
with a single encoded keyframe (an independently decodable frame) per 166 frames, and the decoder started over from
scratch on every access — 44 to 180 ms for each frame missing from the cache. Hence three decisions:

- **pre-decode in a single sequential pass** around τ, about twenty frames on
  each side on desktop (seven on a constrained GPU), interruptible within one
  frame as soon as the playhead moves again;
- a **reduced-resolution proxy** of the whole clip, built in the background
  within a fixed memory budget when its size allows, shown during the drag if
  the frame is already available, then replaced by full resolution as soon as
  the playhead stops;
- **re-encode the demo clips** with an encoded keyframe every 12 frames, without
  B-frames — half of the problem was in the media, not in the code.

One limitation remains: on a MacBook Air M1 with 16 GB, a scrub can still
freeze for a few seconds when an imported clip has a long GOP, or when unified
memory is full of textures. Rather than pretending, the timeline shows a
"rendering… 1.2 s" indicator as long as the engine is waiting for a frame,
with the phase in question. A frozen viewport reads as a wait, not as a
crash — and the number tells you where to look.

## Keyframes you grab with the mouse

Video morphing only makes sense if the landmarks follow the subjects: a point
placed on the chin at second 1 must still be on the chin at second 3. The
landmarks therefore carry **per-side keyframes**, in each video's local time,
and the sampling interpolates between them — for all types, points, segments,
polylines and regions.

The first interface was a list of numbers in the inspector: "0.10 1.10 2.10
2.79…". Unreadable past ten keyframes, and above all blind: nothing connected
those local times to the playhead. The timeline now follows the convention of
animation software. The keyframes of the selected landmarks appear as
**diamonds on the corresponding media track**, converted to master time:
the media time, minus the in-point in the source, plus the clip's offset on
the timeline. The diamond under the playhead is filled; a click jumps to it; a
drag retimes it, snapped to the frame, as a single undoable action; Alt-click deletes
it. When packed too closely together, they merge into a bar with a count instead of becoming unreadable. The
inspector, for its part, shrinks to one line per side: previous, set or remove
here, next.

One design detail deserves telling, because it comes from a real
misunderstanding. On two still images, moving a polyline vertex at τ = 0 and
then at τ = 2 created no keyframe: they only existed in the presence of a
video, and the interface did not say so. Rather than enabling auto-keying
everywhere — and turning every touch-up into an unintended keyframe — the
rule for still images became explicit: a landmark stays static until you have
set a first keyframe by hand; after that, every move at another time adds one.
Which opens, along the way, a possibility still images did not have:
**non-linear morph trajectories** — a banana that passes through an
intermediate shape before becoming a pineapple. Between two keyframes, the
interpolation stays linear; it is their sequence that draws the detour. On a
video, the question does not arise: every move sets a keyframe, since the point
is to follow the subject.

### Presets that really follow the subjects

A few points placed at the beginning, the middle and the end are not enough
for a singing face. The lips change shape between two poses, the head turns,
the shoulders move. To redo the video examples, I prepared the landmarks **on
every source frame**, with local scripts: Apple's Vision framework for the 44
face points and the human pose, then OpenCV to track the robot from
hand-corrected poses. The human body detector got its metallic silhouette
wrong too often; the optical tracking is checked in both directions and falls
back to an interpolation between manual poses when it is not reliable.

These scripts are used to build the presets; the studio loads their keyframes
and lets you retouch them. The result also reminded me of a limit of 2D
morphing: aligning the joints does not make the doubled contours of the
clothes disappear, and pairing the lips does not solve every difference in
hairstyle or occlusion. You have to look at the rendered sequence, frame by
frame, not just a nice screenshot at 50%.

### Retouch a shape, not fifteen vertices

The **Push points** tool moves the handles around the cursor together, with a strong
influence at the center of the brush, decreasing toward the edge. Its radius
is measured on screen, to keep the gesture predictable when you zoom. It acts
on the visible landmarks of the active layer and respects their locks. On an
animated shape, it starts from the position displayed at that instant:
correcting a jaw does not bring it back to its initial geometry. The whole
drag counts as a single undo. These are small choices, but they make the
manual retouching of correspondences much less laborious.

## Learning inside the studio

A grid of algorithms does not tell you where to click. The studio therefore
ships sample projects and a **contextual assistant**, available in sixteen
languages. Each step points to the relevant control. Some detect when the
action is complete and turn green, but wait for a click on "Next" to
continue. "Show me" opens the right panel without touching the project.

The real problem with a tutorial is that it becomes outdated. The steps point at
`data-assist` anchors placed in the interface, and tests check that no
referenced anchor has disappeared and that none is orphaned. That does not
guarantee the explanation is right. It guarantees it points at a button that
exists.

### Translate, then proofread inside the studio

The translation covers the controls, the examples and the guides. It was done
in four passes: translation from a shared glossary, review for accuracy of meaning, editorial
proofreading, then checking in the interface. Graphics vocabulary holds a few
traps: here, *feature* means a landmark, and *feather* the softening of a
mask's edge. Translating them out of context can change the meaning of a
setting.

The last pass also concerns buttons too narrow for their labels, plurals, fonts and
right-to-left Arabic. The panels follow the language; the timeline keeps its
reading direction. Tests check the keys and the variables of the sixteen
catalogs. The English names of the landmarks and the project's custom names
stay unchanged when you switch language. These checks do not replace a review
by native speakers, which remains to be done.

## Exporting: the same loop, frame by frame

The export re-implements nothing: it replays **the same WebGPU render loop** as
the preview, frame by frame, in "exact frame" mode. Each frame becomes a
`VideoFrame`, goes into a WebCodecs `VideoEncoder` (H.264, VP9 or AV1 depending
on the capability probe), and the stream is muxed into **MP4 or WebM** with
[Mediabunny](https://github.com/Vanilagy/mediabunny). The WebCodecs-free
alternative exists: a **PNG sequence** appended progressively into a ZIP,
lossless and transparent — video, on the other hand, is flattened onto a black
or white background.

The hardening lesson, learned the hard way: **validate before allocating**.
Export limits are checked *before* any GPU allocation — 16 to 3,840 px wide,
12 to 60 fps, 15 seconds max, 900 frames, 1 to 20 Mbit/s, a cumulative budget
for PNGs. Exports are **cancellable** at any time, backpressure keeps the queue bounded (encoding
cannot build up frames faster than the muxer can consume them), and encoders, textures,
frames and download URLs are released explicitly. On desktop, a failed export
is annoying; on an iPad, a leaked `VideoFrame` is a tab killed by the OS.

<Callout type="info" title="War story: the videos that did not work on Zen Browser">

On Zen Browser, videos simply did not work. The browser, based on Mozilla's
Gecko engine, did expose WebGPU and WebCodecs. The failure occurred at the point where they connect: in the version tested, `copyExternalImageToTexture` rejected `VideoFrame`
and `HTMLVideoElement` sources with a `TypeError`. Decoding a video frame was
possible; transferring it directly into a GPU texture was not.

The [fix](https://github.com/AndreFrelicot/morpher91/commit/52fdbdda6549823f0880dac6e0647807fde808c4)
adds a detour through an intermediate canvas: the video frame is drawn onto
it, then that canvas is copied to the texture. The canvas is reused, and the
failure of the direct transfer is remembered per GPU device and per source
type, so as not to trigger the same exception on every frame. Browsers that
accept the direct transfer keep it.

Testing for the presence of both APIs at startup was therefore not enough: the
operation that links them had to be checked too.

</Callout>

## Surviving on iPad

Per-pixel morphing is exactly the kind of load that heats up a tablet. Three
decisions emerged from that battle:

- **The GPU budget is not the visual breakpoint.** The "constrained GPU"
  profile is detected through capabilities (`pointer: coarse`,
  `maxTouchPoints`) and caps the render resolution — independently of the
  layout being displayed. An iPad showing the desktop interface keeps its
  tablet budgets.
- **A single resolution cap for every surface.** Rendering, overlays and debug
  views apply the same rule, lowered on constrained devices. Nothing clever,
  no allocator: one rule, everywhere.
- **Never a synchronous snapshot.** The path that synchronously captured a
  full-resolution PNG on mobile view changes froze the interface dead —
  removed, like the mask readback.

The project's qualification reports say it in black and white: 526 tests in
99 files, typecheck, lint, build and responsive simulations all green, but **a
Chromium simulation proves neither real WebKit, nor gestures, nor memory, nor
heat**. A first manual pass on iPad has since confirmed that the app holds up
there. The smartphone, for its part, got a franker answer: the mobile shell
exists, but the experience is **not supported** there. A visitor on a phone
sees a screen that says so clearly, can copy the link to open it on a
computer, and can still push through anyway — a dead-end screen is hostile,
and the line between a foldable phone and a small tablet is blurry.

<Callout type="info" title="War story: the validator that refused the app's own files">
  These reports are there to keep us honest, and the
  pre-release audit caught the kind of bug you never find by clicking. The new
  project validator refused to reopen about **1%** of the files the app had
  itself just saved: trim the start of a clip, save, reopen, "invalid
  structure", file permanently unreadable. The cause came down to a single comparison.
  The guard checked that `start + duration` does not exceed the media's total
  duration, to within `Number.EPSILON`. But the normalizer computes precisely
  `duration = total − start`, and in float64 the reverse sum can land one ulp
  above the total. `Number.EPSILON` is the gap between two doubles around 1;
  as soon as the total exceeds 2, an ulp is worth twice that, and the
  tolerance no longer covered a simple rounding error. Reproduced on 1.04% of
  realistic pairs, fixed with a proportional tolerance. A check meant to
  protect the user was locking them out of their own work.
</Callout>

## 1991 in a tab

Comparing the four algorithms explains part of the result. The rest is worked
out in the layers: choosing the landmarks of a zone, masking what spills over,
shifting its dissolve, checking the seam with the neighboring zones. A single
shot often needs several layers, and the studio keeps them separate until the render:
you redo the mouth or the background's timing without redoing the rest. That
piece-by-piece retouching is what interests me most in Morpher91.

1991 ran on Silicon Graphics workstations. 2026 fits in a tab. Between the two
there is not just Moore's law: there is WebGPU, WebCodecs, and the slightly
stubborn idea that a movie special effect can go back to being a toy you play
with.
